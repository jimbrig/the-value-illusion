1  The Law

The dumps and the design argument are the same conversation seen at two distances.

Up close, R is a heap of tagged nodes, a generational collector, a reference count, a promise, and a handful of objects that really are mutable. From a package away, the useful question is which of your names are allowed to be those mutable objects, and which names are facts that must survive being passed around, printed, tested, and serialized.

Hold both distances at once and a law falls out. It is not an R law. It is a law about programs that have to stay honest while the data is still being discovered.

1.1 Name it

A name is a decision that a concept exists.

api_config is a decision. color is a decision. status_code is a decision if a bare integer would lie. The ubiquitous language Eric Evans described in Domain-Driven Design for large domains is the same discipline scaled down to one package, or one person’s system: the words in the code, the words in the console, and the words in your head are the same words, and you curate them. You do not inherit a vocabulary by subclassing someone else’s nouns. You write one, and you keep it small enough to defend.

Naming is also the encapsulation. A prefix, a package namespace, and a stable verb are how a reader finds the operation. api_config_new, req_headers, format.foo — the module is the vocabulary, not a living object you have to open to discover what it can do. Shells have worked this way for decades. So do the R APIs that people actually love to call.

1.2 Admit only valid values

A name that accepts anything is a comment.

The standard worth stealing is Alexis King’s, from Parse, don’t validate: construction is the check. Downstream code receives a value that already means the name, and it stops re-asking. In a language with a real type checker this is a theorem. In R it is a ritual you can house in an S3 constructor (new_*, validate_*, as_*) or in an S7 property and a class validator. The housing is a tradeoff, discussed in Chapter 12. The requirement is not. If the work is production work, invalid states are a bug at the boundary, not a vibe you check in the third method that happens to care.

R will not make you do this. That is the whole difficulty, and it is also why the REPL is such a good place to discover which names deserve the ceremony. Discovery first. Then the parse.

1.3 Show it well

A type is not done when it can be constructed. It is done when a human can see what it means.

R already treats the console as a user interface: print, format, str, and the semantic vocabulary in cli. PowerShell went further and made the view a separate, declarative contract (format data, sitting on the Monad manifesto’s rule that the pipe carries objects and the host decides how they look). The idea travels even though the XML does not. Skipping format is the same kind of unfinished as skipping the validator: the value exists, and nobody can read it.

Effects need the same courtesy. A warning, a progress meter, a verbose trace, and a fatal error are different channels. Smashing them into cat() throws away the only protocol R has for routing them. That protocol is the condition system, and it is closer to Common Lisp than the tryCatch habit suggests. Chapter 7 goes into it.

1.4 Extend it with verbs

The operation is the extension point.

R’s generic-function tradition (S3, S4, S7) and the trait tradition in Rust share a posture: a new package can teach an old verb a new noun, or teach an old noun a new verb, without owning the type’s method table. Message-passing objects invert the posture. The type owns the verbs, and extension leans on subclassing or patching. Both are legitimate. They fit different problems. Data work, HTTP, compilers, and tool-using models are usually algebras — verbs closed over types — and they want the open verb. Widgets, documents with identity, and long-lived sessions sometimes want the closed object. R lets you build either, which is freedom and also how a codebase loses the plot.

The practical shape that keeps showing up:

Values in the middle. Handles at the rim, where identity is load-bearing: a socket, a token that refreshes, a cache, a conversation. Verbs everywhere a human or a pipe might call them. Views at the host.

1.5 Two sentences, one book

Chambers compressed S and R into three principles, restated in S, R, and Data Science: everything that exists is an object, everything that happens is a function call, and interfaces to other languages are part of the language. The law above is what those principles feel like when you are the one shipping the package.

The metal chapters show why the value illusion holds, and where it is contractually false. The hybrid chapters show why the language grew this shape and why rigor feels like swimming upstream. The taste chapters are the law applied: containers, runtime types, presentation, dispatch.

If you remember one recommendation, remember the split. Facts are values. Capabilities are handles. Functions are the user interface. The console is a view, not a leftover.
