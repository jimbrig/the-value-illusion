14  Data Drives the Design

The machinery is secondary. OO or functional, compiled or collected, checked by rustc or by a constructor: those are how you pay for a smaller set of demands.

Names. A curated vocabulary. Domain-driven design’s ubiquitous language, scaled to a package or to one developer’s system, not reserved for a program with the word “enterprise” in the brief. The names are the model. If you cannot say the sentence without inventing a noun, the model is missing a noun. If you have three nouns for one fact, the model is avoiding a decision.

Types. A name admits only valid, structured values. The structure is known. The meaning is the name, not the payload. An integer64 that is secretly a double is a tolerated lie at the physics layer; at the vocabulary layer the name still has to mean a 64-bit integer, and the verbs have to keep the lie from leaking. A status that is secretly any string is a lie at the vocabulary layer, which is worse, because nothing downstream can recover the five legal values by inspecting the SEXPTYPE.

Views. How a value appears is configurable in the best systems and hand-written in R, and in both cases it is part of the contract. The view is not the value. Format at the host.

Verbs. Functions do the work. Open dispatch attaches them to types without trapping them inside types. A workflow is a composition of verbs that values flow through. When a step needs identity — a socket, a token, a conversation — the value flowing through is a handle, and the handle is explicit.

Data first. The shapes in the domain drive the types. You do not start from a class hierarchy you admired in another codebase and pour the data in. You start from the record, the enum, the document, the event, and you ask what must be true of each. The functions arrive when the shapes can survive a round trip through a verb without losing a law.

Everything earlier in the book is a detail under this picture.

The header, the three empties, cons against vectors, reference counts, ALTREP, and promises explain why the values in the middle can be cheap and still behave like values. The context chapter explains why a handle is a different node, not a different mood. Signals explain how failures and narration stay structured while the values move. The lineage explains why the picture feels natural in R and slightly uphill to enforce. The container rules are the picture applied to one API. The type chapter is the picture applied to a language that will not write the boxes for you. The appearance chapter is the view box, which people skip and users feel.

14.1 What to stop arguing about

Whether a given package is “really OO” is not a useful argument. Whether its names are curated, whether its constructors parse, whether its printers tell the truth, and whether its verbs are open where a stranger should be able to extend them: those are useful arguments. You can win them in S3, in S7, in Rust, in PowerShell, in a disciplined corner of Python. You can lose them in all five, loudly, with a lot of classes.

Composition over inheritance falls out of the same picture. Inheritance bundles “what I am” with “what I can do” and with “how I am stored.” The picture wants those separable. A subtype is a real tool when the domain has a real inclusion and the laws of the parent are laws of the child. It is a bad tool when what you wanted was a new verb, or a new view, or a nullable field. R’s generic functions make the good case easy and the bad case unnecessary. You can still build the bad case. The language is not going to slap the metaphor out of your hand.

14.2 The stance, small enough to reuse

Treat R as a Scheme-powered array language for having a conversation with data. Let the conversation discover the nouns. Then make the nouns boring: parsed, printable, and passed to verbs. Keep mutation in handles at the edge. Keep foreign runtimes behind interfaces that exchange objects and lifetimes, not surprise text. Pick one story for failure at each boundary.

The value illusion is what makes the middle of that program pleasant. You get to think in values while the runtime shares, delays, and sometimes never allocates. The illusion is not permission to forget the edge. At the edge, identity is real, the network is real, missingness is real, and the user’s eye is real. Name those too.

You were not messing around without a goal. You were checking, from the node header upward, whether a taste you already had was compatible with the machine you actually run. It is. The machine will not write the program in that taste. It will only stop punishing you for it, provided you stop asking a value to be a handle, a signal to be a string, and a name to be a hope.
